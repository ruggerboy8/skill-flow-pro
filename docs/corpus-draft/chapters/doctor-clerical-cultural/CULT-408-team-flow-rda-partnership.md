# CULT-408 Team Flow & RDA Partnership

**Domain:** Cultural | **Role:** Doctor | **Tagline:** [ALCAN] "Let the RDA guide the day"

## Overview

Team Flow & RDA Partnership is the richest of the doctor's Cultural competencies. It covers how the doctor and the RDA team share the day: the RDA guides patient flow and formally presents each patient, the doctor trusts RDAs to deliver routine post-op instructions, and the doctor protects the schedule with hard time disciplines (about five minutes of doctor time per routine exam, no more than 20 minutes of treatment time under nitrous, no more than 40 under oral sedation) plus warm redirection of drifting parent conversations. It applies to every Alcan doctor.

## Why it matters

[ALCAN] Two threads run through the framework's rationale:

- **Empowerment and trust.** RDAs have real-time awareness of who has arrived, who is ready, and where the doctor is needed next. When doctors let RDAs guide flow, patients are seen promptly and in the right order, and each empowered team member makes the whole team function better (move 4026). A clear chairside handoff reinforces teamwork, professionalism, and trust in front of families, validates the assistant's role, and even helps the AI documentation system capture key visit elements accurately (move 4027). Repeating instructions an RDA already gave can unintentionally signal to families that the team is not aligned (move 4029).
- **Time as the scarcest resource.** Exam time is the doctor's scarcest resource and the entire schedule is built on it; five focused minutes, delivered consistently, is what keeps every other family's wait short (move 4045). Nitrous gives a limited cooperation window (move 4046), oral sedation has a depth and duration window (move 4047), and parent conversation is some of the highest-value time in the visit, which is exactly why it deserves protection (move 4048).

## The moves

### 4026 - I always allow RDAs to guide the flow of the day.

[ALCAN] From the description: trust RDAs to manage timing, readiness, and transitions, and to direct you to the next patient, so every team member can focus on their role.

Practical guidance, from the What Good Looks Like block:

- [ALCAN] Consistently defer to RDAs to guide patient flow; go directly to the operatory or hygiene chair when the RDA signals a patient is ready.
- [ALCAN] Engage with the child and parent after the RDA's brief presentation; move through the schedule with clear roles and mutual trust rather than independently jumping between rooms.

### 4027 - I always allow the RDA to formally present the patient and guardian to me before diving into the exam/treatment

[ALCAN] From the description: pause and let the RDA formally present the patient and guardian at the chair for both new patient and recall exams. The presentation provides the key clinical details so the team moves smoothly from room to room while staying aligned.

Practical guidance, from the What Good Looks Like block:

- [ALCAN] Wait at the chair while the RDA presents the patient, guardian, medical status, and primary concern in front of the family.
- [ALCAN] Thank the RDA and build on the information; ask follow-up questions that are targeted and additive, not repetitive.
- [ALCAN] The handoff also lets the AI system capture key information for accurate documentation.

### 4029 - I always trust the RDA to provide clear and appropriate post-operative instructions.

[ALCAN] From the Why It Matters block: RDAs are trained to deliver routine post-op instructions clearly and consistently. Delegating lets the doctor focus on reassurance, questions, and next steps. For more complex cases, such as oral sedation or general anesthesia, it is appropriate for the doctor to personally review key post-operative expectations. When RDAs are new, model, observe, and coach until they deliver instructions confidently.

### 4045 - I always keep routine exams to about five minutes of doctor time.

[ALCAN] From the Why It Matters block: the five minutes are not about rushing. Families remember whether the doctor felt present and attentive, not how long the doctor stayed. A prepared, focused exam actually feels more attentive than a long, wandering one, because every minute of it is spent on them.

Practical guidance, from the What Good Looks Like block:

- [ALCAN] Enter the room already briefed by the RDA, so exam time is spent examining rather than orienting.
- [ALCAN] Perform a focused, thorough exam, give the parent a clear purposeful summary, hand off follow-up details to the RDA confidently, and stay warm and unhurried in manner even while efficient in fact.

### 4046 - I always keep nitrous appointments to no more than 20 minutes of treatment time.

[ALCAN] From the Why It Matters block: beyond roughly 20 minutes of treatment time, a child's tolerance falls and the appointment begins costing more than it delivers. The time boundary is really a planning discipline: procedures are chosen and sequenced to fit the window, rather than the window stretched to fit the plan.

Practical guidance, from the What Good Looks Like block:

- [ALCAN] Plan procedures that realistically fit 20 minutes; have the room set up so treatment begins promptly once nitrous is effective; watch elapsed time; defer remaining treatment rather than pushing a struggling child past the window.

### 4047 - I always keep oral sedation appointments to no more than 40 minutes of treatment time.

[ALCAN] From the Why It Matters block: working past oral sedation's depth and duration window trades safety margin and the child's experience for a few more minutes of production. Forty minutes keeps sedation visits predictable, and like the nitrous boundary it is enforced at the treatment-planning stage, not by the clock alone.

Practical guidance, from the What Good Looks Like block:

- [ALCAN] Scope sedation visits, typically one quadrant of involved treatment, to fit 40 minutes; sequence the most important treatment first so a pause never sacrifices the priority; monitor sedation level and comfort against elapsed time; stop or defer rather than working past the window, and explain the reasoning to the parent.

### 4048 - I always keep parent conversations purposeful, warmly redirecting when they drift.

[ALCAN] From the Why It Matters block: parent conversation is where trust, understanding, and case acceptance actually happen. A conversation that drifts stops serving the family in front of you and starts costing the next one. Warm redirection is a genuine skill: the parent should feel they had the doctor's full attention, and the conversation should still land where the child's care needs it to.

Practical guidance, from the What Good Looks Like block:

- [ALCAN] Give genuine undivided attention while the conversation serves the child's care; notice drift early and redirect with warmth; close every conversation with a clear next step; make families feel heard in less time, not less heard in the same time.

## Scripts and examples

**Letting the RDA guide flow (move 4026):** [ALCAN]
> "Who's ready for me next?"
> "Just let me know when the next patient is ready and I'll head over."

**The chairside handoff (move 4027):** [ALCAN]
> RDA: "Dr. Alex, this is Sarah. She's here today with her Mom, Mrs. Smith and this is the 2nd time you've seen them. Her Medical History is significant for Asthma, and mom's main concern is sensitivity on the upper right."
> Doctor: "Thank you. Sarah, and Mrs. Smith, it's great to see you again. Let's take a look together."

**Delegating post-op instructions (move 4029):** [ALCAN]
> Routine: "The RDA will go over the full post-op instructions with you before you leave. You're in great hands."
> Sedation or complex care: "After sedation today, I'll review the most important things to watch for, and then the RDA will go through the full instructions with you before you head home."
> Coaching a new RDA: "Why don't you go ahead and review the instructions with them, and I'll listen in."

**The five-minute exam handoff (move 4045):** [ALCAN]
> "Here's what I'm seeing today, and here's what I'd recommend. Jenni will walk you through the details and get you scheduled. You're in great hands."

**Pausing at the nitrous window (move 4046):** [ALCAN]
> "We got the most important tooth taken care of today, and he did beautifully. We'll take care of the other side at the next visit while he still has a great experience to remember."

**Pausing at the sedation window (move 4047):** [ALCAN]
> "We completed everything we planned for the lower right, and that was the priority. She's had a long appointment, so we'll stop here and finish the other side at her next visit."

**Warm redirection (move 4048):** [ALCAN]
> "That's a great question for Jenni, and she'll take good care of you on it. Before I head to my next patient, let me make sure the plan is clear for you."
> "I want to give that the attention it deserves. Let's make sure we get the treatment plan settled first, and then Jenni can follow up with you on the rest."

## What good looks like / common pitfalls

**Good looks like:** the sourced bullets under each move above.

**Common pitfalls** [ALCAN where drawn from the Gut Check blocks, otherwise GENERAL]:

- [ALCAN] Micromanaging the flow, jumping between rooms independently, or getting pulled into side tasks instead of checking in with the lead RDA (4026 Why It Matters and Gut Check).
- [ALCAN] Diving into the exam before the RDA finishes presenting, or repeating questions the family just answered (4027 Gut Check).
- [ALCAN] Re-delivering post-op instructions the RDA already gave, which slows the visit and signals misalignment (4029 Why It Matters).
- [ALCAN] Re-doing preparation work the RDA already did during an exam, letting exams wander long, or letting families experience hurry instead of focus (4045 Gut Check).
- [ALCAN] Treating a nitrous or sedation overrun as bad luck when it was really a planning miss, or trying to finish rather than pause when a child is struggling (4046 and 4047 Gut Checks).
- [ALCAN] Letting a parent conversation run while the schedule slips, instead of redirecting warmly (4048 Gut Check).

## FAQ seeds

1. **Doesn't a five-minute exam feel rushed to families?** [ALCAN] The framework answers this directly: families remember whether the doctor felt present and attentive, not how long the doctor stayed. A prepared, focused exam feels more attentive than a long, wandering one.
2. **What if the RDA's presentation misses something I need?** [ALCAN] Ask targeted, additive follow-up questions after acknowledging the presentation; the pitfall is repeating what was already covered, not filling genuine gaps.
3. **When should the doctor personally give post-op instructions?** [ALCAN] For oral sedation and general anesthesia cases, the doctor personally reviews the key post-op expectations, then the RDA gives the full instructions.
4. **What do I do when a nitrous or sedation appointment hits its time boundary with treatment unfinished?** [ALCAN] Stop or defer, front-load the priority treatment so a pause never sacrifices it, and explain the reasoning to the parent using the provided scripts.
