# CASE-411: Values-First Discovery

**Role:** Doctor | **Domain:** Case Acceptance | **Tagline [ALCAN]:** "Ask priorities before presenting options"

## Overview

This competency is the front door of the doctor's case acceptance work: before presenting any treatment options, find out what matters most to this family. The three moves form a sequence: ask the values question first (4035), align the recommendation with the answer (4036), and when cost surfaces as a barrier, ask about budget and whether the family wants to see every option, including uncovered ones (4053).

It applies to every doctor, in every treatment conversation, and most importantly when there are areas of concern to discuss.

## Why it matters

[ALCAN] From the framework's Why It Matters blocks:

- Families make decisions through the lens of their values and priorities. When doctors move straight to treatment recommendations without understanding what matters most, even a good clinical plan can feel misaligned or overly directive. Pausing to ask first improves trust and case acceptance while still allowing the doctor to guide care according to clinical standards. (Move 4035)
- When families hear their priorities reflected in the treatment plan, trust increases and resistance decreases. Alignment does not mean compromising the standard of care; it means framing appropriate options through the lens of the family's priorities and being transparent when certain values cannot override medical necessity. (Move 4036)
- Cost is the most common unspoken driver of declined and delayed care, and families rarely raise it twice. Asking about budget respects the family's reality; asking whether they want to see every option respects their autonomy. Some families want only what is covered; others would gladly consider more and are never offered the chance. The only way to know is to ask. (Move 4053)

## The moves

### 4035: Ask what matters most, before options

**Statement [ALCAN]:** "I always ask parents 'what matters most to you about your child's dental care and the experience they have with us'- Before Discussing Treatment Options"

[ALCAN] Guidance from the framework: use this question when discussing areas of concern or treatment decisions. Listen without interrupting, reflect the parent's priority back, and frame recommendations in a way that aligns with what they said. The gut check keeps it honest: did I actually ask, or did I assume? And if the parent seems hesitant later, return to understanding their priorities.

### 4036: Align the recommendation with the family's priority

**Statement [ALCAN]:** "I always align treatment recommendations with what the family identifies as most important."

[ALCAN] Guidance from the framework: name the family's priority out loud before presenting the plan, connect the recommendation to it, offer clinically appropriate alternatives when possible, be transparent when medical necessity limits the options, and close by confirming alignment. The gut check test: if I were the parent, would I recognize my concern in this recommendation?

### 4053: When cost is a barrier, ask about budget and full options

**Statement [ALCAN]:** "When a parent communicates that cost is a barrier to care, I always ask whether there is a budget we should be mindful of, and whether they want to see every option we would recommend, including those insurance may not cover." *(conditionally applicable)*

[ALCAN] Guidance from the framework: treat a cost signal as planning information, never something to gloss past. Ask the budget question plainly and warmly, ask whether the family wants to see all options, make no assumptions from appearance, insurance type, or payment history, and carry the answers into how the plan is framed and sequenced.

## Scripts and examples

All quoted from the framework's doctor script resources [ALCAN]:

**Opening the values conversation (move 4035):**
> "There are a couple areas I'm concerned about that I'd love to talk through with you and discuss some options for. But before we do that, it helps me to understand: what matters most to you about your child's dental care and the experience they have here?"

> "Some families prioritize comfort, others want to be very proactive, and some care most about efficiency or minimizing visits. Do any of those resonate with you, or is there something else that matters most?"

> "Thank you for sharing that. Knowing that comfort is most important to you helps me explain the options in a way that fits what you're looking for."

**Reflecting the priority into the plan (move 4036):**
> "Based on what you shared, that comfort is really important to you, I'd like to talk through sedation options that can help make this experience easier for your child."

> "Since you mentioned wanting to avoid as much treatment as possible, let's talk about the options that address the problem while keeping things as conservative as we can."

> "I want to honor what's most important to you. At the same time, there are situations where certain medical needs still require us to recommend treatment to protect your child's health."

> "Does this approach feel like it aligns with what you shared matters most to you?"

**Meeting the cost signal (move 4053):**
> "I'm really glad you mentioned that. Would it help to talk through this with your budget in mind? Is there a number we should be mindful of?"

> "Some families want to see every option we'd recommend, even ones insurance may not fully cover. Others prefer to start with what's covered. Which would be more helpful for you?"

> "Thank you for telling me. That helps me put together a plan that actually works for your family."

## What good looks like / common pitfalls

[ALCAN] Sourced from the What Good Looks Like and gut check resources:

- The values question comes before treatment options, the parent is heard without interruption, and the conversation feels collaborative rather than directive.
- The recommendation names the family's stated priority and the parent would recognize their concern in it.
- Cost signals get engaged directly, with no judgment spoken or felt, and the plan conversation actually uses what the family shared.

[ALCAN] Pitfalls implied by the gut checks:

- Assuming the family's priority instead of asking.
- Framing the plan around what is easiest for the doctor rather than what matters to the family.
- Moving past a cost mention, or letting assumptions about appearance, insurance type, or payment history stand in for the question.

[GENERAL] A pitfall worth watching in any values-first approach: asking the question but then presenting the same one-size-fits-all plan anyway. Parents notice when the answer changed nothing.
