# CASE-412: Comfort, Safety & Sedation Guidance

**Role:** Doctor | **Domain:** Case Acceptance | **Tagline [ALCAN]:** "Safety-driven sedation recommendations"

## Overview

This competency covers how doctors guide families through sedation decisions and the emotions that come with them. Four moves: offer and consider general anesthesia for the children who genuinely need it (191), recommend the sedation modality that is safest and most likely to succeed while honoring family priorities (4037), run a brief pre-treatment check-in with the parent before care begins (4038), and when a parent looks overwhelmed, name it, normalize it, and ask an open question (4055).

It applies to every doctor planning or delivering sedation-supported care. The clinical dosing floor for oral sedation lives in CLIN-404 (move 205); this competency is about choosing the modality and carrying the family through it.

## Why it matters

[ALCAN] From the framework's Why It Matters blocks:

- Considering GA for complex pediatric cases ensures optimal treatment delivery while prioritizing safety and comfort. It minimizes psychological trauma, reduces the risk of complications from in-office sedation failures, and allows comprehensive care in a single visit, reducing the burden on patient and family. (Move 191)
- Families rely on the doctor's expertise to recommend the safest, most comfortable, and predictable experience for their child. Offering sedation that is predictable to fail can lead to treatment trauma, incomplete care, or escalation during the appointment. (Move 4037)
- Even when treatment has already been discussed, parents often arrive uncertain about how the visit will go. A brief pre-treatment check-in prevents surprises, sets expectations that outcomes may depend on findings or cooperation, and reinforces that we will not force a child through treatment if cooperation becomes unsafe or overly distressing. (Move 4038)
- An overwhelmed parent has stopped absorbing information, and decisions made in that state do not hold. They resurface later as cancellations, no-shows, and second-guessing. Naming the moment, staying deliberately vague about the emotion, normalizing, and asking an open question hands the parent the floor. (Move 4055)

## The moves

### 191: Offer and consider general anesthesia when indicated

**Statement [ALCAN]:** "I always offer and consider general anesthesia as a treatment modality for extensive treatment needs (3+ quads) children with behavioral concerns, or medically complex children who are high risk candidates and not suitable for oral sedation, or based on parent preference for GA."

[ALCAN] Guidance from the framework: thoroughly assess medical history and current behavior; offer stepwise care (nitrous, oral sedation) when it is safe and appropriate; correctly identify non-compliant patients who are highly unlikely to tolerate other modalities, to minimize failed appointments; and clearly communicate benefits, risks, and alternatives. A well-run practice on this move shows a low rate of failed appointments or repeated sedation attempts for patients ultimately referred to GA.

### 4037: Recommend what is safest and most likely to succeed

**Statement [ALCAN]:** "I always recommend sedation options based on what is safest and most likely to succeed for the child, while also considering what matters most to the family."

[ALCAN] Guidance from the framework: begin with a clear professional recommendation and its reasoning, then discuss other clinically appropriate options if the family wants to explore them. Ask what matters most before discussing options (this is Values-First Discovery applied to sedation). Avoid recommending a modality when cooperation level or treatment complexity makes failure likely, and avoid steering away from a modality just because you are personally uncomfortable with it when it may be appropriate. Document the clinical reasoning for the sedation plan.

### 4038: Pre-treatment check-in with the parent

**Statement [ALCAN]:** "I always perform a brief pre-treatment check-in with the parent to confirm the plan, set expectations, and reinforce our commitment to the child's comfort before beginning care."

[ALCAN] Guidance from the framework: keep it to 1 to 2 minutes at the start of treatment appointments. Confirm today's plan, proactively mention reasonable variations (deep decay can require additional treatment, occasionally extraction; sedation can still leave a child anxious, in which case we may pause or stop), reinforce that comfort and cooperation guide the visit, and end with an invitation for questions.

### 4055: Name overwhelm, normalize it, ask an open question

**Statement [ALCAN]:** "When a parent seems overwhelmed by findings or a plan, I always name what I am noticing out loud, normalize it, and ask an open question about what is going through their mind." *(conditionally applicable)*

[ALCAN] Guidance from the framework: watch for the signals (silence, a glazed look, rapid-fire questions, a change in posture), pause the clinical content because more information is the wrong medicine in that moment, name what you are noticing tentatively without labeling a specific emotion ("this might be a lot" lands whether the parent is anxious, upset, or processing; "I can see you're scared" can feel invalidating if it misses), normalize in the same breath, ask an open question, then wait through the silence. Resume the plan only once the parent has re-engaged.

## Scripts and examples

All quoted from the framework's doctor script resources [ALCAN]:

**Recommending GA (move 191):**
> "Given the extent of treatment needed and [child's specific needs/age], I recommend general anesthesia to ensure we can complete all necessary work comfortably and safely for [child's name] in one visit."

> "Considering [child's] medical history and their ability to cooperate, general anesthesia will provide the safest and most effective environment for their dental care."

**Recommending the modality (move 4037):**
> "Based on the amount of work we need to do and how your child is doing today, my recommendation would be oral sedation."

> "The reason I recommend that is because it gives us the best chance of a smooth and comfortable appointment for your child."

> "Some families ask about doing this with nitrous alone. With the amount of work needed, nitrous by itself often isn't strong enough to keep children comfortable the whole time."

> "Oral sedation works very well for some children, but when kids are very anxious or the treatment is more involved, it can sometimes be unpredictable. In those situations, I usually recommend [GA / deeper sedation] so the experience is smoother for the child."

**The pre-treatment check-in (move 4038):**
> "Before we get started today, I just want to take a quick minute to review the plan with you."

> "Once we remove the decay, sometimes teeth that are very deep can require additional treatment, and occasionally extraction can be the healthiest option. I just like parents to know that possibility ahead of time."

> "We're using oral sedation today to help your child relax. Most children do very well with it, but occasionally kids can still feel anxious. If that happens, we may need to pause or stop treatment, because we always prioritize your child's comfort and safety."

> "Before we get started, what questions do you have?"

**Meeting overwhelm (move 4055):**
> "I can see this might be a lot to take in, and that's completely normal. Can you tell me a little about what's going through your mind?"

> "Most parents feel that way when they hear this much at once. Take your time."

## What good looks like / common pitfalls

[ALCAN] Sourced from the What Good Looks Like and gut check resources:

- GA candidates (extensive or complex restorative needs, severely pre-cooperative patients, special healthcare needs) are identified consistently, with empathetic explanations that leave parents understanding and comfortable with the plan.
- The sedation recommendation leads with safety and predictability, is defensible from a safety and success standpoint, and its reasoning is documented.
- Every treatment appointment opens with a concise, confidence-building check-in that covers the plan, reasonable variations, and the comfort commitment.
- Overwhelm gets noticed and met before the clinical download continues.

[ALCAN] Pitfalls implied by the gut checks:

- Recommending nitrous or oral sedation when cooperation level suggests it will fail, setting the child up for a traumatic or failed appointment.
- Avoiding a modality out of personal discomfort even though it may be appropriate for the child.
- Skipping the check-in because treatment was "already discussed" at planning.
- Labeling a parent's specific emotion instead of naming the moment tentatively, or plowing ahead with the plan while the parent has stopped absorbing.
