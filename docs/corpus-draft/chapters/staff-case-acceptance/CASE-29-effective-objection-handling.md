# CASE-29 Effective Objection Handling

Role: Dental Assistant (RDA) | Domain: Case Acceptance | Framework code: RDA.CASE 1

## Overview

This competency belongs to Dental Assistants (RDAs). [ALCAN] It is about skillfully addressing patient objections to recommended treatments, such as concerns about cost, urgency, or convenience, by reinforcing the clinical necessity and benefits, and helping families understand why the treatment is important for their child's health.

[ALCAN] The framework's tagline: "Overcome 'no'."

## Why it matters

[ALCAN] "You help them see the value. When you gently address a concern about treatment, you act as a partner in their long-term health."

[GENERAL] The RDA is usually chairside when hesitation first surfaces, often before the doctor or treatment coordinator ever hears about it. That makes you the person best positioned to find out what the worry really is, respond to the right thing, and hand the conversation off well. Objection handling here is listening first, reassuring second, and looping in the right teammate every time.

## The moves

### Move 31: Reassure, then loop in the doctor

[ALCAN] "When a parent is uncertain about a procedure, I always reassure them about the importance of the procedure, and inform the doctor about the conversation."

[ALCAN] Parents worry because they care. Offering calm reassurance and looping in the doctor keeps communication clear and helps families feel supported. The coaching note: validate, don't argue. Say "It makes sense that you are worried about [X]. Here is why we recommend it..." and connect it to safety.

### Move 68: Link treatment to the parent's goal

[ALCAN] "I always link the dentist's recommended treatment to the parent's desired outcome for their child."

[ALCAN] Parents care most about the end result; linking the treatment to their goals helps everything make sense. When you make that connection clear, it builds trust and keeps the path forward focused on what matters to them. The coaching note: connect the dot to their goal. "You said you wanted to avoid pain for him? This filling stops the pain before it starts."

### Move 73: Find the real concern first

[ALCAN] "When hesitation surfaces, I always identify the primary concern, comfort, time, or cost, so I can respond to the right thing."

[ALCAN] You're likely the first person to hear about a worry. That means you're the most likely to understand exactly where it's coming from. The coaching note: don't be afraid to slow down, ask questions, and help the parent feel heard about whatever their concern is.

### Move 85: Cost concerns, staged plans, and the treatment coordinator

[ALCAN] "When cost is a concern, I explain that the doctor has prioritized what's most important to do first but that we can plan the rest in safe stages. I communicate the worries with the treatment coordinator."

[ALCAN] Cost worries are normal. Staying calm and explaining that the doctor has already prioritized the safest order helps families feel supported, not pressured. Handing off to the treatment coordinator keeps the conversation clear, kind, and organized. The coaching note: don't negotiate clinical need. Validate the cost, then hand off. "I know it's an investment. Let's have the Treatment Coordinator see how to fit this into your budget."

### Move 131: X-ray objections

[ALCAN] "I always communicate the importance of X-rays to our standard of care any time a parent objects or attempts to decline them."

[ALCAN] X-rays help the doctor catch things we can't see with our eyes. Explaining that clearly, and kindly, helps parents understand that imaging isn't optional; it's part of providing complete and safe care. The coaching note: to see is to know. Explain "We can't fix what we can't see. The X-ray ensures we don't miss a cavity hiding in between."

### Move 163: Communicating the value of zirconia crowns

[ALCAN] "When discussing Zirconia crowns with a guardian, I always communicate their value by highlighting that we're unique in placing them, their improved appearance, and their long term durability."

[ALCAN] We believe in this treatment, even if insurers don't. So we need to be able to explain why, and why the family should care.

**Linking to the parent's goal (move 68):**

**Cost concern with staging (move 85):**
> "Dr. Alex put this in order of what needs to happen first, and we can definitely do the rest in stages. Our treatment coordinator will walk through the options with you before you leave, how does that sound?"

**X-ray objection (move 131):**
> "X-rays help us find things we can't see from the surface, so they're incredibly important for keeping your child's care safe and accurate. If you have questions or concerns, we can certainly wait and ask Dr. Jess when she gets here."

## What good looks like / common pitfalls

[GENERAL] Good looks like: you name the concern out loud before answering it, your reassurance ties to the child's health and comfort, and the doctor or treatment coordinator always hears about the conversation, so the family never has to raise the worry twice.

[GENERAL] Common pitfalls:
- Answering the wrong objection (reassuring about pain when the real worry is cost).
- Debating price or discounting clinical need chairside instead of handing off to the treatment coordinator.
- Reassuring a parent but never telling the doctor the conversation happened.
- Turning reassurance into pressure; the goal is a supported decision, not a cornered one.

## FAQ seeds

**Q: A parent wants to skip the X-rays. What do I say?**
A: [ALCAN] Use the move 131 script: X-rays help us find things we can't see from the surface, they are part of safe and accurate care, and the doctor can address specific concerns when they arrive.

**Q: A parent says they can't afford the whole plan today. Can I offer to break it up?**
A: [ALCAN] Yes, in the framework's shape: explain the doctor has prioritized what needs to happen first and the rest can be planned in safe stages, then connect them with the treatment coordinator and pass along the worry.

**Q: Why do we recommend zirconia crowns when insurance may not pay for them?**
A: [ALCAN] Because we believe in the treatment: we are unique in placing them, they look like a real tooth, and they are durable long term. Our doctors place them all the time.
