# CLIN-1 Patient Flow Coordination

Role: Front Desk (DFI) · Framework code: DFI.CLIN 1 · Tagline: "Keep the lobby flowing"

## Overview

[ALCAN] This competency is about managing the check-in and check-out processes smoothly so patients move efficiently through the front office, adjusting to real-time changes in arrivals and departures to keep flow organized and waiting times short.

It applies to everyone in the Front Desk (DFI) role. As the framework puts it: "You are the conductor of the front office! By keeping check-ins and check-outs smooth, you ensure every patient feels cared for and no one is left waiting."

## Why it matters

[ALCAN] The front desk is where the day either flows or jams. The framework's language is consistent on the theme of the handoff: "The baton pass matters. If you click Checked In but don't tell the back, the baton hits the floor." Silence toward a waiting family "feels like indifference to a waiting parent." The DFI is described as "the air traffic controller" and "the backup engine" for the office.

[GENERAL] In a pediatric practice, waiting compounds quickly: an anxious child gets more anxious, a parent's patience wears down, and one stalled check-in can back up the whole clinical schedule. Small, immediate communication habits at the desk are cheap insurance against all of that.

## The moves

### Move 4: Alert the back the moment a patient is checked in

[ALCAN] "I always actively alert the clinical team that a patient is ready to be pulled back directly after changing patient status to 'Checked In'."

[ALCAN] The second you hit "Checked In," tell the back. Clicking the status alone is not the handoff; walk back there or use the chat immediately. It is a small habit that keeps patients moving and the day flowing.

### Move 17: Escalate waits longer than 10 minutes

[ALCAN] "I always notify the practice manager then Lead RDA immediately if a patient has been 'Checked In' and waiting for longer than 10 minutes."

[ALCAN] When wait times stretch, act fast and loop in the right people early, before a delay becomes a problem. You are the alarm. The framework suggests asking the Practice Manager directly: "How do you prefer I alert you when we hit the Red Zone?"

### Move 54: Sequence simultaneous arrivals

[ALCAN] "When two or more families arrive within 3 minutes, I always note arrival order and suggest a pull order to the clinical team ('A then B') using the tablets to keep the lobby moving."

[ALCAN] Don't just check families in; sequence them. Explicitly tell the back: "Family A arrived first, please seat them before Family B."

### Move 74: Write the board and hand it off

[ALCAN] "I always write name, age, and any patient portion on the board after finishing check-in, and hand it directly to an available RDA."

[ALCAN] Keep the board clean and color-coded; it is how the team stays synced. Always hand it off so the back knows a patient is ready.

[ALCAN] "When the treatment coordinator is occupied I always step in and handle any payment of outstanding balance using the card reader."

## What good looks like / common pitfalls

[GENERAL] What good looks like:

- Status changes and human alerts happen as one motion, never as two separate tasks.
- The lobby never holds a mystery: the DFI always knows who has waited how long and the back always knows who is ready.
- Simultaneous arrivals get an explicit order rather than leaving the back to guess.
- Payment moments never stall a family at the desk because only one person can run the reader.

[GENERAL] Common pitfalls:

- Clicking "Checked In" and assuming the software told somebody ([ALCAN] the framework calls this dropping the baton).
- Waiting for a parent to complain before acknowledging a delay.
- Leaving the board half-filled or stale so the back stops trusting it.
- Treating the card reader as someone else's machine.
