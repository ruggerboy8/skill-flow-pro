# CLIN-20 Patient Comfort and Communication During Procedures

> **DRAFT - AI-generated 2026-08-20 from the Pro Moves framework. Not canon. Requires review by Ariyana before entering the Ask corpus.**

Role: Dental Assistant (RDA) · Framework code: RDA.CLIN 4 · Tagline: "Keep patient calm"

## Overview

[ALCAN] This competency is about continuously centering the patient's experience throughout dental procedures: actively monitoring comfort, understanding, and emotional state; keeping communication open; providing reassurance; explaining steps when appropriate; and promptly addressing any signs of pain, discomfort, or fear. Prioritizing comfort and engagement during treatment reduces interruptions and improves clinical outcomes.

It applies to Dental Assistants (RDAs). The framework's framing: "You are the patient's advocate in the chair. Monitoring their comfort and easing their fears turns a scary appointment into a safe one." The Lead Dental Assistant carries a coaching version of this same competency (see CLIN-554).

This is the richest RDA clinical competency in the framework: seven moves spanning the whole arc of an appointment, from first touchpoint through post-op instructions, four of them with sourced scripts.

## Why it matters

[ALCAN] The framework names the core mechanic of pediatric fear directly: "Surprise equals fear. Never touch without talking." Comfort monitoring is framed as advocacy: "Watch their hands. If they clench, touch their shoulder and remind them to breathe. Don't just watch the tooth." Positive reinforcement works in real time: "Catch them being good... It reinforces the behavior you want." Handoffs and previews build trust because "it shows the team is aligned," and the RDA owns the outcome end to end: "even though the doctor performs the procedure, it's your patient."

[GENERAL] A calm child is also a safer, faster appointment: less squirming during sharp-instrument moments, fewer stops and restarts, and a family more likely to come back without a fight. Comfort work is clinical work.

## The moves

Arranged roughly in appointment order.

### Move 157: Preview the doctor and the procedure before treatment

[ALCAN] "Before treatment begins, I always preview the doctor, the procedure, and invite any questions or concerns from the parent or patient."

[ALCAN] Kids relax when they know what is about to happen, and parents trust you when you take time to include them. A calm preview sets the tone, and the answers you get are a pulse check you can relay to the doctor before they walk in.

### Move 127: Play the correct Parent Pre-Treatment video before the doctor arrives

[ALCAN] "I always play the correct Parent Pre-Treatment video prior to Dr. arrival in the room."

[ALCAN] The video makes sure parents understand their role during the treatment and helps prevent interruptions. "Let the screen do the teaching" while you set up the tray.

### Move 126: Present the patient when the doctor enters

[ALCAN] "When the doctor enters, I always present the patient by name, state the planned procedure, share relevant medical history, and relay any parent questions or concerns."

[ALCAN] A smooth handoff helps the doctor focus immediately and shows the family that everyone is on the same page. Summarize to build trust, stating the plan clearly in front of the parent. (The doctor-track mirror of this move is CULT-408's "I always allow the RDA to formally present the patient." The Office Manager observes this hand-off weekly under CLIN-35.)

### Move 22: Tell, show, do

[ALCAN] "I always tell, show, do before performing any action on a patient."

[ALCAN] Kids feel safer when they know what is coming. Showing and explaining each step builds trust, reduces fear, and helps the visit go smoothly. Never touch without talking. The framework's example "tell" line for the suction: "It's just a thirsty straw that makes a slurping sound."

### Move 59: Affirm specific positive behaviors

[ALCAN] "I always notice and verbally affirm specific positive behaviors during clinical activities."

[ALCAN] Don't wait for the end of the appointment. Say "Great job holding open" every 60 seconds or so; it reinforces the behavior you want while it is happening.

### Move 98: Monitor comfort and manage stress throughout

[ALCAN] "I always monitor patient comfort, scanning for breathing, tension, and signs of anxiety, and narrate progress or use distraction to reduce stress, unless the doctor is already managing comfort, in which case I support their lead."

[ALCAN] Comfort cues are easy to miss. Noticing a child's breathing or body tension early lets you step in before anxiety builds. If the doctor is already leading comfort, your role is quiet support: matching tone and reinforcing calm.

### Move 195: Deliver post-op instructions and expectations

[ALCAN] "After treatment I always share clear instructions and set expectations for patients and parents about numbness, food/drink to avoid, and reasons to call in case of concern."

[ALCAN] You are the conductor: you make sure parents and patients understand what they need to for the best post-op experience possible. The framework urges practicing this out loud with a teammate. (The doctor-track mirror: CULT-408's "I always trust the RDA to provide clear and appropriate post-operative instructions.")

## Scripts and examples

Four moves carry sourced scripts.

[ALCAN] Pre-treatment preview and pulse check (script resource for move 157):

> "Ok, so Dr. Alex will be starting that extraction in a second. Before I go and get her, can you tell me how you're feeling about the procedure today?"

[ALCAN] Presenting the patient to the doctor (script resource for move 126):

> "Dr. Alex, Max is 5 years old and is here today with his mom, Mrs Smith. This is the 2nd time we've seen Max and he's in today for his check up and cleaning. Max has a history of asthma and uses an inhaler when he is sick. Mrs. Smith mentioned that she is concerned he hasn't lost any teeth and would like you to check on that. Max did great for his cleaning, x-rays were a little hard for him to bite on but I'm really proud of him for trying today! I am sure you remember that Max loves monster trucks, so I told him you were going to check and see if there were any monster truck tracks in his mouth today!"

Notice the structure packed into that example: name and age, who is with the patient, visit history, today's procedure, medical history, the parent's specific concern, behavior notes with praise, and a personal connection point.

[ALCAN] In-procedure reassurance (script resource for move 98, punctuation lightly adapted):

> "You're doing great. We're almost through this next part. If you need a break, just let me know."

[ALCAN] Post-op instructions (script resource for move 195, "For a Filling" and "For an Extraction"):

> For a filling: "Ok Mrs. Jackson, Dr. Alex just got done doing a filling. Your son is going to experience some numbing in that area for a little while; that's totally normal. As soon as the that numbness wears off, he can eat and drink like normal. If Jason experiences any excessive pain or discomfort, that'd be a good idea to call us right away to get taken care of."
>
> For an extraction: "We did an extraction today, they'll be numb for about another hour, please watch them to make sure they don't bite themselves. While the extraction is healing, avoid straws or spitting really hard. Bleeding has stopped for now but if there is any increase in bleeding, please go ahead and call us right away so that we can make sure that he's ok."

[ALCAN] Tell-show-do "tell" line for suction (from move 22's coaching): "It's just a thirsty straw that makes a slurping sound."

[GENERAL] One unsourced example of the hand-raise pause offer that pairs naturally with these moves (the sourced version lives in CULT-26, move 61): "If you ever need me to stop, just raise your hand like this and I'll pause right away."

## What good looks like / common pitfalls

[GENERAL] What good looks like:

- The child hears about every sensation before feeling it; nothing in the appointment is a surprise.
- Praise is specific and continuous ("great job holding so still") rather than one generic "good job" at the end.
- The doctor walks into a room that is already calm, informed, and set up, and gets a complete patient presentation in front of the family.
- The RDA's eyes move between the tooth and the child: hands, breathing, shoulders.
- The family leaves able to repeat the post-op plan back (the share-back invitation is its own move in CASE-30, move 77).

[GENERAL] Common pitfalls:

- Narrating for the parent's benefit in adult clinical language that means nothing to the child.
- Going quiet during the tense stretch of a procedure, exactly when narration and distraction matter most.
- Competing with the doctor's comfort management instead of supporting their lead ([ALCAN] the move itself names this boundary).
- Rushing post-op instructions at the door while everyone is packing up, then hoping the printout covers it.

## FAQ seeds

**Q: What does "tell, show, do" actually look like in practice?**
A: [ALCAN] Before any action on a patient: say what you will do, show it in a non-threatening way, then do it. The framework's example: introducing the suction as "a thirsty straw that makes a slurping sound" before it goes in.

**Q: What belongs in the patient presentation when the doctor walks in?**
A: [ALCAN] Patient name, planned procedure, relevant medical history, and any parent questions or concerns. The move 126 script shows the full form, including behavior notes and a personal connection point.

**Q: What if the doctor is already managing the patient's comfort?**
A: [ALCAN] Support their lead: quiet support, matching tone, reinforcing calm, rather than running a parallel comfort track.
