# CULT-26 Demonstrating Emotional Intelligence (Dental Assistant / RDA)

> **DRAFT - AI-generated 2026-08-20 from the Pro Moves framework. Not canon. Requires review by Ariyana before entering the Ask corpus.**

## Overview

This competency is the RDA's emotional radar: reading the family's mood, giving the child control, and managing your own stress so it never reaches the patient. The framework tagline: "EQ on display." The Office Manager has a leadership-level version of this in CULT-43.

## Why it matters

[ALCAN] The framework defines it as exhibiting "high emotional intelligence by effectively managing personal emotions and recognizing and responding appropriately to the emotions of patients and colleagues," fostering "positive interactions and a supportive environment within the practice."

[ALCAN] The friendly description: "You read the room perfectly. Managing emotions, yours and theirs, creates a supportive atmosphere where everyone feels safe."

[GENERAL] In a pediatric operatory, emotions are clinical data. An anxious child cooperates less, an anxious parent hears less, and a stressed assistant transmits both. The three moves here cover all three directions: theirs, the child's, and yours.

## The moves

### 1. Assess the family's mood at "In Chair" and quietly brief the doctor (action 24)

[ALCAN] "I always take a moment to assess the parent's and patient's mood after changing status to 'In Chair,' and I quietly share any concerns with the doctor."

[ALCAN] Framework guidance: "Reading the room early helps the doctor understand what kind of visit it may be. Quietly passing along concerns allows the doctor to adjust their approach with care and sensitivity." Coaching note: "Read the room. If the parent looks anxious, whisper a heads-up to the Doctor before they enter. It helps them adjust their entry energy."

[GENERAL] This pairs with the doctor-side expectation that the RDA formally presents the patient before the exam begins (see the Doctor chapter CULT-408); a mood heads-up is part of a good presentation.

### 2. Give the patient a stop signal before any clinical activity (action 61)

[ALCAN] "Before beginning a clinical activity I tell the patient that they can raise their hand to ask me to pause at any time."

[ALCAN] Framework guidance: "Giving kids permission to pause puts them back in control. It lowers fear, builds trust, and helps the whole visit feel more predictable." Coaching note: "Control reduces pain. Tell the child: Raise your left hand if you need a break. It makes them feel safe enough to cooperate."

### 3. One deep breath before you speak or act when your stress rises (action 83)

[ALCAN] "When I notice my own stress rising, I always pause for one deep breath to center myself before speaking or acting."

[ALCAN] Framework guidance: "A quick reset protects the room from tension. One calm breath helps you show up with patience, clarity, and kindness, even in busy moments." Coaching note: "You can't pour from an empty cup. If you are frustrated, turn away for 3 seconds, breathe in for 4, out for 4. Reset before you re-engage."

## What good looks like / common pitfalls

[GENERAL] Good looks like: the doctor never walks into a tense room blind; every child hears the stop-signal offer before instruments come near them, and the signal is honored instantly every time; on chaotic days the RDA's voice and pace stay the same as on calm days.

[GENERAL] Common pitfalls: announcing family concerns in front of the family instead of quietly; offering the stop signal but then talking the child out of using it, which teaches them the signal is fake; mistaking your own hurry for the schedule's need and letting it show in your hands and tone.
