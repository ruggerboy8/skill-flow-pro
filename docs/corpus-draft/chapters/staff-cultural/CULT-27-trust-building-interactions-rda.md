# CULT-27 Trust Building Interactions (Dental Assistant / RDA)

## Overview

The RDA's trust-building competency covers the chairside welcome: the greeting, the curious question, the goals conversation with new families, and the comfort setup (coffee, blanket, headset, show). The framework tagline: "Earn their trust." It is the richest Cultural competency for the RDA role, with five moves.

## Why it matters

[ALCAN] The framework defines it as building "genuine connections with patients through personalized interactions," making patients "feel known and appreciated by remembering details, addressing their individual needs, and maintaining professionalism, thereby fostering trust and a sense of being truly cared for."

[ALCAN] The friendly description: "You are the face of care. Remembering personal details and treating patients like friends builds a trust that keeps them coming back."

[GENERAL] The RDA spends more one-on-one minutes with the child than anyone else in the visit. A child who trusts their assistant tolerates more, fears less, and comes back happier, and a parent who watches that happen becomes the practice's biggest advocate.

## The moves

### 1. Greet by name, smile with eye contact, introduce yourself (action 20)

[ALCAN] "I always greet the patient by name, smile with eye contact, and introduce myself by name."

[ALCAN] Framework guidance: "The first moments set the tone. A warm greeting with eye contact helps the child feel safe and helps parents relax, knowing you're attentive and present." Coaching note: "A name is a safety signal. Look at the chart, say the name out loud to yourself, then go get the patient. Make eye contact immediately."

### 2. Ask a curious open-ended question while seating (action 57)

[ALCAN] "I always ask the patient a curious open-ended question as I am seating them."

[ALCAN] Framework guidance: "Simple, curious questions help kids feel seen and ease into the visit. It turns the room into a friendlier place and helps you build trust early." Coaching note: "Distraction starts with connection. Ask about a pet, a sport, or a shoe color. Any topic other than teeth helps the child settle in."

### 3. Ask the five-year goals question at new patient appointments (action 105)

[ALCAN] "I always use the question 'What are your goals for your child's oral health over the next 5 years?' during a new patient appointment."

[ALCAN] Framework guidance: "Asking about goals shows the parent that their priorities matter. It gives the doctor helpful context and helps families feel like true partners in their child's care."

[GENERAL] Pass the answer along: mention it in your patient presentation so the doctor can build on it (the doctor-track discovery moves in CASE-411 pick up exactly this thread).

### 4. Offer parents a coffee and patients a blanket before procedures (action 121)

[ALCAN] "I always offer parents a coffee and patients a blanket prior to all procedures."

[ALCAN] Framework guidance: "Small gestures make big impressions. Offering comfort items helps families relax and shows that you're thinking about their experience, not just the procedure." Coaching note: "Comfort comes first. Before you lean the chair back, offer a blanket. It takes 10 seconds and buys you 10 minutes of cooperation."

### 5. Set up the headset and let the patient pick what to watch (action 122)

[ALCAN] "I always set patients up for comfort by offering a headset and asking them what they'd like to watch."

[ALCAN] Framework guidance: "Helping the patient choose something to watch gives them control and distraction during the visit. It turns an unfamiliar procedure into something familiar and safe." Coaching note: "Give them control. Letting the child pick the show gives them agency in a scary environment. Do this before you open any instruments."

**Comfort offer** (script for action 121): "Can I get you a coffee? And would you like a blanket while we wait for Dr. Eduardo?"

[GENERAL] Curious seating question: "So what's the best thing that happened at school this week?" or "Who's this on your shirt? Tell me about them."

## What good looks like / common pitfalls

[GENERAL] Good looks like: the child hears their own name before they hear any dental words; the walk back to the operatory is a conversation, not a procession; the comfort setup (blanket, headset, chosen show) is complete before any instruments are opened; the parent's five-year goals reach the doctor.

[GENERAL] Common pitfalls: leading with the clipboard instead of the greeting; asking closed questions that die in one word; skipping the comfort offers on busy days, which is when they matter most; letting the goals answer stay in your head instead of reaching the doctor; picking the show for the child to save time, which gives away the agency the move is designed to create.
