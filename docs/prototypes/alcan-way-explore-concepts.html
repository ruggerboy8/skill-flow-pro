The Alcan Way on a phone: three concepts

Three interaction models for the Alcan Way pillar of the new Explore tab, each built from the locked 13-beat sequence: the journey stages showing excellence in action, with each beat pairing the visible moment against the role-specific move behind it. Every concept answers the same six questions: how you enter and re-enter from a plain tab, how stages and beats are staged, how dialogue gets said instead of skimmed, how the principles and your own role's moves surface, what finishing leaves you with, and how a lead points someone at a single beat.

The Walk

A pocket film, one card per moment

Optimizes for

The first full watch-through. One beat per full-screen card shows excellence in order, and every card pairs the visible moment with the role-specific Pro Move behind it, so the "what" and the "how" arrive together. It honors the client's swipe instinct and translates the desktop's out-and-back camera into a depth strip you can feel. Interruptions are cheap: it reopens on the card you left, remembered on the device, nothing stored anywhere else.

Costs

Linear by nature, so jumping to one beat mid-coaching takes a beat index rather than a natural gesture. Dialogue is heard and echoed more than rehearsed. Needs all 13 cards staged with art before it feels finished.

The Table Read

A script you perform, not a story you watch

Optimizes for

Dialogue as the product. The lines in the beat map are meant to be said in a real lobby, so this concept stages the Way as a five-scene script where your role's lines are highlighted, insights are director's notes in the margin, and the ending hands you your sides: every line that's yours, on one card. Strongest for coaching and rehearsal, and the fastest surface for a two-minute revisit before a meeting.

Costs

Deliberately gives up the pixel art and the scene-craft, so it teaches the moves better than it shows the experience whole. Needs per-role line mapping, and beats with no dialogue (the arrival, the walk back) become stage directions rather than moments.

The Floor

Walk the floor plan, stop by stop

Optimizes for

Role legibility and coaching. The office itself is the interface: an out-and-back path through the lobby, hallway, and exam room, with each beat as a numbered stop colored by the role whose move it is, so a front desk person, an assistant, and a doctor each see their own stops at a glance. Every stop is a place with a stable link, so "go look at the walk-out beat" is literal. It's stateless by design: the map is a table of contents you can walk in order or dip into, nothing to track. At the last stop the lights go out, one phone glows with the review, and the question turns to your role: which of these stops is yours tomorrow?

Costs

First-timers can wander out of order and blunt the arc, so the first walk needs a gentle "in order" default. The map is the hardest artwork and layout in the set, and dialogue lives one tap deep inside sheets rather than in your face.
